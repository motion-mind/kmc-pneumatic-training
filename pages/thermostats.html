Unit 02

Pneumatic thermostats

A pneumatic thermostat is the master controller of the loop. It senses room temperature and puts out a 3–15 psi branch signal that tells the box controller (or a heating valve) how much to do. Get the thermostat right and the box has a chance; get it wrong and no amount of box calibration will save the zone.

What it does

Inside the thermostat, a temperature-sensing element — a bimetal, a sealed vapor-pressure bellows, or a liquid-filled element — moves a flapper over a bleed nozzle fed through an internal restrictor. Exactly the mechanism from Unit 01. As the element moves, branch pressure varies proportionally. That branch signal leaves the thermostat and becomes the control command for the rest of the loop.

Types you will meet

Single-temperature, single-acting

One setpoint, one output. The classic VAV cooling-only thermostat. Direct acting in most cooling applications.

Dual temperature (heating/cooling)

Two outputs, one for heating and one for cooling, sequenced with a deadband between the setpoints. Often used with a heating coil or reheat in the zone.

Day/night (setback) thermostat

Has a second main-air connection. When the night line is energized (usually by a time clock or solenoid), the effective setpoint shifts to the night value.

Aspirating thermostat

Contains a small fan that continuously draws room air across the sensing element for faster, more representative response. Needs its own power or an air-driven aspirator.

Single-pipe

One tube; the restrictor is inside. Common on older installations. Read branch on that same line.

Two-pipe

Separate supply and output. Simpler to diagnose because main and branch can be measured independently at the device.

KMC pneumatic thermostats

KMC's pneumatic thermostat families include the CTC-1600 / CTC-1611 / CTC-1612 series and the CTE-5100 / CTE-5101 series. Like all pneumatic thermostats they have a setpoint dial and a calibration (zero) adjustment, and their installation guides cover mounting, connections, adjustments, and calibration. Always pull the exact model's installation guide before adjusting — the cover comes off differently and the calibration screw is in a different place from one family to the next.

Installation and location

No calibration can compensate for a bad location. The thermostat reads the air at its sensing element.

  • Mount on an interior wall, at roughly breathing height, where room air circulates freely.
  • Avoid exterior walls, direct sunlight, supply diffusers, door drafts, and corners that stagnate.
  • Keep clear of heat sources: copiers, coffee makers, monitors, ballasts, and equipment that cycles on and off.
  • Do not bury a thermostat behind furniture or a partition, and do not box it in with an enclosure that changes how it samples air.
  • Make sure tubing is not kinked at the wall penetration, and leave a service loop.

Calibration

Calibration answers one question: when the room is at the dial value, is the branch at mid-scale? For a 3–15 psi signal, mid-scale is about 9 psi.

  1. Confirm main air before anything else. A thermostat cannot be calibrated against a weak or fluctuating supply.
  2. Let the sensor stabilize in the space. Give it time — a thermostat that just came off a cold truck or a warm hand will lie to you.
  3. Measure true room temperature with an accurate thermometer near the thermostat, at the same height.
  4. Connect a 0–30 psi gauge to the branch output (or tee into the line), so you can watch the signal.
  5. Set the dial to the measured room temperature, so the sensed value and the setpoint agree.
  6. Adjust the calibration screw until the branch reads mid-scale (about 9 psi). This is the zero adjustment — it shifts the whole curve without changing its slope.
  7. Verify the action and range. Warm the sensor (or set the dial lower) and confirm the branch moves the full range in the correct direction. If DA is expected, output should rise as temperature rises; if RA, it should fall.
  8. Check the throttling range. Confirm the thermostat is not so narrow that it hunts or so wide that it responds sluggishly. Match the device's stated range.
  9. Record the result on the commissioning sheet: room temperature, dial setting, branch pressure, action, date, technician.
Why mid-scale?

At mid-scale the thermostat has equal authority to increase or decrease, so it can respond to a disturbance in either direction without running out of signal. Calibrating at an extreme cripples half the control band.

Calibration vs setpoint

The calibration screw moves the curve. The dial moves the setpoint. If you are tempted to crank calibration just to change the temperature the space holds, stop — that is the setpoint's job, and using calibration to make up for a location or load problem hides the real fault.

Day/night (setback) adjustment

On a day/night thermostat there are effectively two setpoints. To set it up:

  1. Calibrate in day mode first. Verify the day setpoint and branch mid-scale with the night line at its day pressure.
  2. Energize the night line as the time clock would (or apply the night main pressure per the device).
  3. Adjust the night setpoint using the night calibration or the night adjustment screw until the space would hold the desired unoccupied temperature.
  4. Verify changeover. Confirm the shift happens cleanly, and that a failed night line fails to the safe (usually occupied) setpoint rather than driving the space to an extreme.
